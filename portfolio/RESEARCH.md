# What makes a creative portfolio convert

Research brief behind the two portfolio sites in this folder. "Convert" here means one thing: a visitor who might hire you sends an inquiry.

A note on evidence: almost nothing about portfolios has been measured in controlled studies. Most of what's published is practitioner advice from designers, recruiters and portfolio-builder companies. Where a number comes from real research it's marked **[data]**. Where it's practitioner consensus it's marked **[consensus]**. Treat consensus as a strong default, not a law.

---

## 1. You get about a minute, so the first screen has to do the selling

- First-pass review is short. One informal study of 16 hiring managers reviewing 243 applications found an average of **55 seconds** to decide, cover letter included **[data, small study]**. Other estimates range from 15 to 120 seconds. The popular "6 seconds" figure has no traceable source, so ignore it.
- A recruiter or client usually decides about a reel in the **first 30 seconds** **[consensus]**.
- People judge credibility by looks. In Stanford's 2002 credibility study (2,684 people, 100 sites), **46.1%** of comments cited the site's design or look **[data]**. For a designer this is doubled: the site itself is a work sample.
- Speed counts before anyone sees anything. Google/SOASTA found bounce probability rises **32%** as mobile load time goes from 1s to 3s, and **123%** from 1s to 10s **[data]**.

**What the sites do about it**
- The first screen says who you help and what you make (a niche headline, not "Creative Designer"), shows work right away, and has one clear button.
- Strongest project goes first, not the most recent.
- No frameworks, no page builders, lazy-loaded images, and video embeds that only load after a click. This keeps the first load small.

## 2. Specialise in the headline

- Clients look for work that looks like their problem. Hiring guides tell clients to check "whether the designer has worked with businesses similar to yours" **[consensus]**.
- For video: "a reel that mixes weddings, corporate and music videos tells a client you are not their specialist" and "YouTube editor for finance creators" stands out more than "video editor" **[consensus]**.
- No study measures how much niching lifts hire rates. It's the most consistent advice across every source, though.

**What the sites do about it**: The hero headline names a client type and an outcome. The work is sorted by the kind of job a client is hiring for (brand identity, packaging, social; YouTube, Shorts, ads).

## 3. Fewer projects, each told as a short story

- **8–12 projects maximum** for graphic design; **3–6 full projects** plus a reel for video **[consensus]**.
- Each case study follows the same shape: **brief → problem → your role → process / key decisions → final work → outcome** **[consensus]**. Two or three short paragraphs plus images is enough.
- The process (sketches, rejected directions, the system behind the logo) is what most portfolios leave out and what clients use to judge how you think **[consensus]**. A red flag for clients: "no explanation of process or results, just pretty pictures."
- Show systems, not single pieces: logo + palette + type + real mockups; a full social campaign rather than 30 unrelated posts **[consensus]**.
- Use a consistent format across case studies, and specific titles ("Mo's World of Scents: launch flyer redesign"), never "Project 3" **[consensus]**.

**What the sites do about it**: The graphic design site has a full case-study page template (`work/mos-world-of-scents.html`) with those sections in that order. Every project card has a one-line outcome under it.

## 4. For video: reel first, short, sound-off friendly, and prove results

- **Reel length: 60–90 seconds** for a website hero; some sources allow up to 2 minutes **[consensus]**. Best clip in the **first 5 seconds**.
- Many people watch on phones with sound off, so burn in captions and lead with visually strong cuts **[consensus]**.
- **Before/after clips** are the most persuasive format because they isolate what the editor changed. Use one or two, not ten **[consensus]**.
- Report **views, retention or conversions**, but only numbers you can back up and have permission to share **[consensus]**.
- Pitch retainers. Monthly packages turn one-off jobs into steady income **[consensus]**.
- Default YouTube embeds pull roughly 0.8–2.6 MB of scripts before anyone presses play. A click-to-load "facade" avoids that; one monitoring dataset reported pages using one had ~800ms faster LCP **[data, single source]**.

**What the sites do about it**: The video site opens with a muted, looping reel preview and a "Watch the 75-second reel" button. It has a raw-vs-edited comparison with a list of exactly what changed, an audience-retention chart, and monthly retainer packages. All players load only on click.

## 5. Make contacting you easy and low-risk

- Typical contact forms convert **1–5%** of visitors; well-optimised ones **8–20%** **[consensus, vendor data]**.
- Fewer fields wins. Reported lifts vary (from cutting 4 fields to 3, up to cutting 11 to 4), but the direction is consistent **[consensus]**. Requiring a phone number is flagged as a major drop-off point.
- The form that works for freelancers: **name, email, project type (dropdown), short message**, plus a note saying how fast you reply **[consensus]**.
- One primary call to action, repeated in the header, mid-page and at the end **[consensus]**.
- Testimonials work best with a **full name, role and business**, placed **next to the call to action** **[consensus]**. Anonymous "Happy client" quotes don't build trust.
- A visible process (what happens after you click) and an FAQ (price, timeline, revisions) remove the reasons people hesitate **[consensus]**.
- Showing a "from" price: no study found. Practitioners use it to filter out mismatched budgets. Both sites include it; delete it if you'd rather quote everything.

**What the sites do about it**: A 4-field form with response time stated, plus WhatsApp as a second option (most clients in Nigeria prefer it to email). The CTA sits in a sticky header (always visible, on phones too), after the work, and at the end. Testimonials sit directly above the contact section.

## 6. Style: let the work be the loudest thing

- **Graphic design site**: a white page, black type and one strong accent. Print-production details (crop marks, colour swatches, spec lines) act as quiet signals of craft. The project images carry the colour.
- **Video site**: dark by design, because footage reads better on a dark surface and every editing tool uses one. Timeline and timecode details come from the editor's own software. Amber is the single accent.
- Both avoid the looks that now read as "AI template": purple-to-blue gradients, emoji section markers, everything centred, generic stock imagery.
